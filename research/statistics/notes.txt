Author: Marlon Dominguez
Date: 08/12/2026

My goal is to describe what happens to the price in greater detail. It should not focus only on the final return or whether a target was eventually reached. The path the price takes and the order in which events occur can be just as important as the final result.

First, we should record which important price level is reached first. For example, does the price reach the midpoint before the trade becomes invalid? Does it return to the entry price before reaching the opposite boundary? Does it achieve a profit of 1R before losing 1R? We should also note whether the price continues moving after a breakout, returns inside the range, retests the broken level, or reaches the stop before the target. This matters because two trades may touch the same levels but produce very different results depending on the order in which those levels are reached.

Although I do would not use time as a contributing factor to a favorable trade I think this would be useful for other reasons. We should also measure how long favorable and unfavorable price movements take. For both possible long and short trades, this includes the largest potential gain and loss, how many bars it takes to reach each one, how quickly the trade first becomes profitable, and how long it remains at a loss. We should record the longest run of unfavorable bars, the return after set periods, and the time needed to reach profit levels such as 0.5R, 1R, and 2R. Two trades with the same final return may feel very different in practice if one becomes profitable quickly while the other spends a long time losing money first.

Another useful measure is how smoothly the price moves toward its objective. We can compare the final move with the total distance traveled, measure how much the price moved favorably compared with how much it moved against the trade, and record how deeply it pulled back before continuing. We can also count changes in direction, calculate how often each closing price moved closer to the target, and measure how long the price remained beyond the broken boundary. This helps distinguish a clean breakout from one that eventually succeeds only after a great deal of back-and-forth movement.

I am mostly curious whether the market truly accepts the new price area or quickly rejects it. We can measure how many candles close outside the original range, how many do so consecutively, how far the price remains outside, and how long it takes to return inside. If the price re-enters the range, we should record how deeply it moves back in, whether it reaches the midpoint, and whether it later breaks through the same boundary again. We should also note whether a retest of the broken boundary holds or fails and compare the time spent inside the range with the time spent outside it.

Breakout strength should also be measured. This includes the greatest distance the price travels beyond the broken boundary, both before and after any retest. That distance should be compared with market volatility and the width of the original range. We can record whether the price moves one-quarter, one-half, one, or two times the range width beyond the boundary. We should also determine whether momentum becomes stronger or weaker after the breakout. The range width is especially useful because it provides a natural estimate of how far the price might move.

Failed breakouts should be examined separately. We should record how far the initial breakout traveled, how long the price stayed outside the range, and how quickly it returned. Once it re-enters, we should measure how deeply it moves into the range, whether it reaches the midpoint or opposite boundary, and whether the original breakout direction later resumes. Repeated cycles of breaking out and returning should also be counted. A small breakout that fails immediately may behave very differently from one that remains outsside the range for several bars before failing.

In regard to invalidations (the events marked with a red X) they should have their own set of outcomes. We should examine where the price moves in relation to the original range, whether it continues immediately after invalidation, how often it returns to or retests the range, and how far it ultimately travels. We should also record how long it takes to revisit the old midpoint, whether it crosses the entire range, and whether a new range forms nearby. Most importantly, we should determine whether invalidation leads to a lasting trend or simply means that the original range has become unreliable.

Different types of invalidation should be kept separate because they may produce very different results. These types include a clear break through a boundary, a gradual weakening while the price remains inside the range, pressure from an existing trend, and situations where the reason for invalidation is unclear.

Also, let's try to evaluate whether each confirmed range creates a genuine trading opportunity. We should record whether either boundary is reached, which boundary is reached first, and how long it takes for the first interaction to occur. We should count the number of usable trading events, determine whether the range becomes invalid before any reasonable entry appears, and note whether both long and short opportunities occur. The length of the active period and the period after invalidation should also be measured. Any estimate of the best available move should begin from a realistic trading signal rather than from a perfect entry chosen with hindsight.

As an extra, after favorable results are observed, trading costs can also be included. A price move that appears profitable before costs may not be worthwhile after accounting for the spread, commission, and slippage. We should calculate the return after these expenses, determine how much favorable movement remains after the entry cost, and identify the minimum move needed to break even. It would also be useful to estimate how much of the apparent advantage is consumed by costs under normal, elevated, and unusually high-cost conditions.

The results are going to be expressed in several comparable units. These may include the raw price move, pips or points, market volatility at the time of entry, the width of the original range, and the initial amount at risk, known as R. If position-sizing rules are available, results can also be shown as percentage returns. Adjusting results for volatility and range width will make it easier to compare events across different markets and market conditions.

I must be careful not to rely only on average results, because averages can hide serious risks. It should show the range of outcomes, including poor, typical, and strong results. We should capture the worst movement against a trade, occasions when the price gaps beyond a possible stop, unusually large losses, and losses that occur even after the setup appears to be confirmed. Losing streaks and the average result among the worst 5% of cases should also be examined. The goal is to find events with manageable downside risk, not events with a casual ppositive average return.

Finally, after favorable observations, clearly identify cases where candle data cannot tell us exactly what happened. Examples include a stop and target being touched within the same candle, several boundary events occurring during one candle, or the market opening beyond a boundary or intended entry. Missing data, irregular timestamps, duplicate signals, overlapping ranges, and invalidation occurring during the same candle as a new event should also be flagged. These situations should not be resolved through hidden assumptions. When the available data cannot reveal the true order of events, the results should show the best-case outcome, the worst-case outcome, and an unresolved classification.
